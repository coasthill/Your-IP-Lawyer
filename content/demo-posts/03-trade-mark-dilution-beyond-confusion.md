Ordinary trade mark infringement is a theory about confusion. The defendant's mark is so similar to the registered mark, and the goods so close, that a customer is likely to believe the two come from the same source or are connected. Take away the likelihood of confusion and the ordinary claim collapses.

Dilution is different. It is a theory about *erosion*. A mark that has become famous carries a value that has nothing to do with any particular purchase: the instant recognition, the single association, the sense that this word means one thing and one thing only. A stranger who uses the same word on unrelated goods may confuse nobody, and may still chip away at that singularity. Dilution law exists to stop the chipping.

## Two ways to dilute

The doctrine is usually described as having two limbs.

**Blurring** is the weakening of a mark's distinctiveness through its use on goods or services that have nothing to do with the original. Nobody buying a bicycle under a famous perfume name thinks the perfume house has moved into cycling. But if the name appears on bicycles, then on paint, then on a chain of restaurants, it slowly stops meaning the perfume.

**Tarnishment** is damage to the mark's reputation by association with something unsavoury, inferior or incongruous. The use need not be on similar goods; it need not confuse. It need only bring the mark into an association its proprietor would never have chosen and which a reasonable customer would find degrading to it.

Indian statute law recognises both. A registered mark with a reputation in India is protected against use on dissimilar goods or services where that use, without due cause, takes unfair advantage of or is detrimental to the distinctive character or repute of the mark. The language is broad. The threshold that gates it is not.

## The threshold is reputation, and it is high

Dilution is not available to every registered proprietor. It is available to marks that have a reputation in India, and, in the way the doctrine has developed, the reputation demanded is considerable. The statute also carves out the category of *well-known marks*, which enjoy protection across all classes of goods and services and can be recorded as such by the Registry.

> A mark that everybody in the trade knows is not the same as a mark that everybody knows. Dilution is a remedy for the second kind.

What has to be proved is recognition among the relevant public, which for a genuinely famous mark means the general public, not merely the customers of the product. Evidence of sales, advertising spend, market share, media coverage, enforcement history and prior recognition by courts or registries all go in. A proprietor with a solid but ordinary brand will usually fail at this stage, and should think hard before pleading dilution at all. A finding that the mark falls short of fame is an unhelpful thing to have on the record.

## What does not need to be shown

Because dilution is not infringement, the familiar machinery of confusion does not apply in the same way. In particular:

- The goods or services need not be similar. Dissimilarity is the premise of the claim, not a defence to it.
- Likelihood of confusion need not be shown. The customer may know perfectly well that the two sources are unrelated.
- The defendant's intention is not decisive. A use may dilute whether or not it was calculated to trade on the mark's fame, though an evident intent to free-ride is rarely ignored.

There is a live question about how close the two marks must be. The confusion-based claim asks whether the marks are deceptively similar. The dilution provision speaks of marks that are identical or similar, and some courts have read that as requiring a closer identity between the marks than the ordinary test, reasoning that when the goods are different, the mark itself must do more of the work. Others have applied the ordinary similarity analysis. The point remains contestable and should be argued rather than assumed.

## "Without due cause" and the room for legitimate use

The statutory protection is against use *without due cause*. That phrase is where much of the defendant's case lives. Descriptive use of a word that happens to be someone's famous mark, honest use of a personal name, comparative advertising within permitted limits, commentary, parody and criticism have all been argued to be uses with due cause. How far parody is protected in India is unsettled, and the answer will often turn on whether the use reads as commentary on the mark or as a way of selling something else with borrowed glamour.

Free-riding, meaning taking unfair advantage of the mark's repute, is the third head, and in commercial cases it is often the easiest to establish. A defendant who chose a famous mark for unrelated goods will struggle to explain the choice as coincidence.

## Passing off across product lines

The common law reaches some of the same territory by a different route. A passing off claim can succeed where the claimant's reputation is so wide that the public would assume a connection even across dissimilar goods. But the claim still requires *misrepresentation*: a likelihood that the public will believe in some connection, sponsorship or licence. Where that cannot be shown, passing off runs out and only the statutory dilution claim remains. The two claims overlap but are not interchangeable, and pleading them as if they were invites the court to treat both loosely.

## Why the doctrine is narrower than it looks

Most demand letters invoking dilution are sent by proprietors whose marks are known in their trade and unknown outside it. Dilution is a remedy for a small number of marks whose fame has become an asset in its own right, and courts are properly cautious about extending to ordinary marks a monopoly that spans every product category.

For the advocate, the discipline is to ask the reputation question honestly before the claim is drafted. If the answer is uncertain, the safer course is usually the ordinary claim, with dilution pleaded, if at all, as a secondary case rather than the lead.[^1]
