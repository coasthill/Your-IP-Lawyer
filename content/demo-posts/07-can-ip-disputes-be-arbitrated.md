Ask whether intellectual property disputes can be arbitrated and you will get two confident answers in opposite directions. One camp says of course: these are commercial disputes between commercial parties, and the arbitration clause in the licence should be honoured like any other. The other says of course not: patents and trade marks are grants of the state, and no private tribunal can decide whether the state was right to grant them. Both camps are right about the disputes they are thinking of. The trick is to work out which dispute is actually in front of you.

## Rights in rem and rights in personam

The organising distinction in Indian arbitrability law is between rights *in rem*, held against the world at large, and rights *in personam*, held against particular persons. Disputes about rights in rem are, as a general rule, not arbitrable, because an arbitral award binds only the parties to it and cannot settle a question that affects everyone. Disputes about rights in personam are arbitrable, subject to statute and public policy. And, importantly, disputes about rights in personam that *arise from* rights in rem are also arbitrable.

Applied to intellectual property:

- The **grant, validity and registration** of a patent, trade mark or design is a matter in rem. Whether a patent should ever have been granted is a question for the Patent Office and the courts, decided with effect against the world. An arbitrator cannot revoke a patent or cancel a registration.
- **Contracts about intellectual property**, such as licences, assignments, franchise agreements, technology transfers and royalty arrangements, create rights in personam. Whether a licensee paid what it owed, whether an exclusivity term was breached, whether a licence was validly terminated: these are ordinary commercial disputes, and an arbitration clause governing them will be enforced.
- **Infringement** sits between the two, and is the hard case.

## The hard case

An infringement claim asserts a right in rem, the proprietor's right to exclude, against a particular person. The relief sought is personal: an injunction against this defendant, damages from this defendant. On the in personam side of the ledger, that looks arbitrable. But the claim exists only because the state granted the right, and the defendant's usual answer is that the right is invalid, which drags the in rem question back in.

The emerging approach distinguishes by relationship. Where the parties are in a contractual relationship, so that a licensee is said to have exceeded its licence or a former licensee to have continued after termination, the dispute is in substance about the contract, and the infringement claim is a consequence of the contractual breach. Courts have been willing to refer such disputes to arbitration. Where there is no contract, and the claim is a pure tort by a stranger, there is nothing to arbitrate under: no agreement, and no basis for a private tribunal to adjudicate a public grant.

> The arbitration clause governs the relationship it was written for. It does not follow the intellectual property out of the contract and into the world.

What an arbitral tribunal can do when validity is raised as a *defence* remains contestable. One view is that the tribunal may form a view on validity for the limited purpose of deciding the dispute between the parties, without any effect on the registration itself. Another is that the moment validity is in issue, the dispute has become non-arbitrable and must be stayed or referred. The first view has more support in international practice; Indian authority is thinner, and the point should be pleaded with care.

## The public-policy boundary

Even where a dispute is in personam, arbitration must yield where a statute confers exclusive jurisdiction on a specific forum, or where the subject-matter engages a public interest that private adjudication cannot serve. Rectification of the register, revocation, compulsory licensing and opposition proceedings are all in this category. They exist to keep the public register accurate and the public domain intact, and they are not the parties' to compromise.

The boundary also affects remedies. An arbitral tribunal can award damages and can restrain a party, by injunction, from conduct in breach of contract. It cannot order the Registry to do anything, and an award purporting to declare a registration invalid with general effect would be unenforceable to that extent.

## Drafting for arbitrability

Most of the disputes that reach a courtroom over arbitrability could have been avoided by better drafting. A few practical points:

1. **Define the scope with intellectual property in mind.** A clause covering all disputes arising out of or in connection with the agreement will usually catch a post-termination infringement claim between the parties; a narrower clause may not.
2. **Carve out what the tribunal cannot do.** Expressly reserve validity, registration and rectification to the competent forum, so that a validity defence does not derail the whole reference.
3. **Provide for interim relief.** Courts can grant interim measures in aid of arbitration, and institutional rules often allow emergency arbitrators. Say which route the parties prefer, because IP disputes rarely wait.
4. **Consider confidentiality.** The privacy of arbitration is one of its main attractions in technology licensing, and the clause should say so rather than assume it.
5. **Think about who is not a party.** Group companies, sub-licensees and distributors will often be the actual infringers. A clause that binds only the signatories may leave the real dispute in court.

## The answer

Can IP disputes be arbitrated? Disputes about the *ownership of the right against the world* cannot. Disputes about *what the parties promised each other* can. Disputes about infringement can where they arise from a relationship the parties chose to govern by arbitration, and cannot where they do not. That is not a fully settled statement of Indian law. It is a reasonable reading of where the authorities stand, and the edges will keep moving.[^1]
