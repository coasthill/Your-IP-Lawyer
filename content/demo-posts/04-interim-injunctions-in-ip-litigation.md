In most areas of civil litigation the interim application is a preliminary skirmish. In intellectual property it is usually the war. If the claimant obtains an injunction, the defendant must rebrand, redesign or withdraw, and the commercial reality changes long before any witness is sworn. If the claimant fails, the defendant is free to trade, and the claimant must decide whether a trial years later is worth the cost. Both sides know this. That is why so much of the effort in an IP suit goes into a hearing that, in theory, decides nothing finally.

## The three limbs

The court asks three questions, and the answers are cumulative rather than alternative.

| Limb | What the applicant must show | Where applications usually fail |
|---|---|---|
| Prima facie case | A serious question to be tried with a real prospect of success. Not proof, but more than an arguable plea. | Weak title; a mark or design that is itself vulnerable; a claim that depends on disputed facts the court cannot resolve on affidavit. |
| Balance of convenience | That the harm to the applicant if relief is refused outweighs the harm to the respondent if it is granted. | The respondent has traded openly for years; the applicant's loss is quantifiable; a restraint would destroy a business to protect a marginal interest. |
| Irreparable harm | That damages would not be an adequate remedy. | The applicant licenses the right for a fee, has tolerated identical use elsewhere, or cannot say what harm is feared. |

*Prima facie case* is the threshold. It is not a mini-trial. The court looks at the pleadings and documents to see whether the applicant has a case that is real rather than illusory, and it will generally not resolve contested questions of fact at this stage. But it is not toothless either. In a patent case, for instance, a credible challenge to validity may mean that the applicant has not made out a prima facie case at all, and courts have been more reluctant to grant interim relief where the patent is recent and untested. Whether recency alone should matter is itself debated.

*Balance of convenience* asks the court to compare consequences. It is here that the size of the parties, the length of the respondent's use, the availability of alternatives and the possibility of tailored relief are weighed.

*Irreparable harm* is often assumed in IP cases, on the reasoning that loss of goodwill, or of the exclusivity that gives a right its value, cannot be measured in money. The assumption is not automatic. An applicant who has licensed the very right in question for a royalty has priced the harm already.

## Delay

Delay is not a fourth limb, but it operates on all three. An applicant who has known of the respondent's use for a long time and done nothing will find it hard to say that the harm is irreparable, and the balance of convenience shifts as the respondent builds a business in reliance on the silence.

Two distinctions matter. The first is between *delay* and *acquiescence*. Mere delay, where the applicant was simply slow, weakens the case for interim relief but does not necessarily defeat the suit. Acquiescence, where the applicant knew, stood by and in effect encouraged the respondent to continue, is a defence to the claim itself. The second is the effect of dishonesty. Courts have been willing to grant relief despite substantial delay where the respondent's adoption of the mark was plainly dishonest, on the footing that a copyist should not profit from the applicant's inattention. How far that principle stretches is contestable, and the safer course for any applicant is simply to move quickly.

## Ex parte relief

An ex parte injunction is granted without hearing the respondent. It is an exceptional remedy justified by urgency and by the risk that notice would allow the respondent to defeat the order, whether by disposing of stock, moving assets or destroying evidence. The court must record its reasons for proceeding without notice.

> An ex parte order is a promise made on one side's word. The applicant's duty of candour is the price of it.

That duty is the central discipline of ex parte practice. The applicant must place before the court every material fact, including facts that hurt: prior correspondence, earlier proceedings, the respondent's known defences, the applicant's own delay. Suppression is a recognised ground for vacating an ex parte order regardless of the merits.

The limits of ex parte relief are structural as well as ethical:

- It is meant to be temporary. Procedural rules require the court to hear the respondent within a short period and to dispose of the application promptly.
- It should be no wider than the urgency demands. A restraint on the specific infringing article is easier to justify than a restraint on the respondent's business.
- It carries risk. An applicant who obtains an ex parte order and later fails may face an order to compensate the respondent for the harm the injunction caused.
- It does not settle the merits. A respondent who was not heard has every right to be heard, and the order may not survive that hearing.

## Practical notes

For the applicant, the strongest interim case is one that is documented, fast and candid. Good title, clear evidence of the respondent's use, a demand letter sent promptly, and an affidavit that anticipates the respondent's answers will do more than eloquence.

For the respondent, the interim hearing is often the only real opportunity to shape the litigation. A credible challenge to validity, evidence of prior use, proof of the applicant's delay or of inconsistent enforcement elsewhere may not win the suit but may prevent the injunction, which, in commercial terms, is usually the point.

Either way, the interim order is a snapshot of the case as it appeared on affidavit. It binds nobody's view of the final result, and advocates who treat it as a verdict on the merits tend to misjudge what comes after.[^1]
