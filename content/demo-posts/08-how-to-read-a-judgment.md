Every year new juniors arrive in chambers able to find a judgment in seconds and unable to say what it decided. This is not their fault. Law school teaches judgments as sources of principle: read for the rule, memorise the rule, move on. Practice uses them as instruments: read for what happened, what was argued, what the court accepted and on what facts, so that the same court can be persuaded again or a different court can be shown the distinction. The two kinds of reading are different skills. This note is about the second.

## Start with the facts, and be suspicious of the headnote

The headnote is somebody else's summary. It is often accurate, sometimes not, and never a substitute. Read the facts first, in the court's own words, and read them slowly. Ask what the parties were actually fighting about. In a great many judgments the principle everyone cites was stated in a case where the principle was not really in dispute, and the case turned on something else entirely.

Facts matter for a second reason. A judgment binds on its facts. When you take a proposition from a case to a court, the first question from the bench will be whether the facts there were like the facts here. If you have not read them, you cannot answer.

## Find the issues

A judgment answers questions. Find them. Some courts list the issues expressly; many do not, and you will have to reconstruct them from the arguments and the structure of the reasoning. Write them down as questions, one line each. If you cannot state the issue as a question that the court answered yes or no, you have not yet understood the case.

## Separate the ratio from the obiter

The *ratio decidendi* is the principle that was necessary to the decision: the rule the court had to adopt in order to reach the result it reached on the facts before it. *Obiter dicta* are everything else the court said along the way: illustrations, hypotheticals, views on questions not in issue, observations about what the law ought to be. The ratio binds subordinate courts and, in the usual way, guides coordinate ones. Obiter persuades, with a weight that depends on the court and the care with which the point was considered.

The distinction is easy to state and difficult to apply, for three reasons:

- Courts do not label their reasoning. You must work out what was necessary by asking whether the result would have been different had the court taken the other view on the point.
- Judgments often give more than one reason. Where each reason is independently sufficient, each is generally treated as ratio; where a reason is offered as an alternative "even if", its status is arguable.
- Broad statements of principle are frequently obiter as to most of their breadth. A court deciding a passing off case between two pharmaceutical brands does not, by stating the test generally, decide how it applies to software.

> When a senior asks what a case decided, the answer is a sentence beginning "On facts where..." and ending with the result. Anything that does not fit that form is probably obiter, or your own gloss.

## Orders and judgments are not the same thing

Juniors are often handed an *order*, whether an interim order, a procedural direction or a short disposal, and asked what it decides. Frequently the honest answer is: very little. An interim order records a prima facie view on incomplete material. It is not a finding, it binds nobody's final conclusion, and citing it as authority for a proposition of law is a mistake that experienced opponents will punish.

A reasoned judgment after hearing is a different creature. It decides. But even then, read the operative part, meaning the actual orders made at the end, against the reasoning. It is not unheard of for the two to diverge slightly, and the operative part is what the parties must obey.

Note also the court and the bench. A larger bench binds a smaller one of the same court. A judgment of one High Court is persuasive, not binding, in another. A single judge's decision may have been appealed; check before relying on it. None of this is pedantry. It is the difference between a proposition the court must accept and one it may politely decline.

## Taking notes for a brief

The purpose of a case note in practice is to let someone who has not read the judgment use it safely in argument. That person is usually your senior, at speed, and often in court. The note should be short and structured. A workable format:

1. **Citation and court**, with the bench strength and the date, and whether it was an interim or final decision.
2. **Facts** in three or four lines, focused on the facts that mattered to the decision.
3. **Issues**, as questions.
4. **Held**, issue by issue: what the court decided, with a pinpoint reference to the paragraph.
5. **Ratio**, in one sentence of the "On facts where..." form.
6. **Useful passages**, quoted exactly, with paragraph numbers, marked as ratio or obiter.
7. **Distinguishing features**: anything about the facts or procedure that an opponent could use to say the case does not apply.
8. **Subsequent history**, if known: appealed, followed, doubted.

The seventh item is the one juniors omit and seniors most need. A case note that says only what helps is half a case note. The bench will find the other half.

## Reading for the other side

The last discipline is to read every judgment twice: once for what it gives you, and once as your opponent will read it. What would you say if the other side cited this? Where is the limiting language? Which facts are unlike yours? If you cannot find anything, read it a third time. There is always something, and the advocate who finds it first controls how the case is used.[^1]
