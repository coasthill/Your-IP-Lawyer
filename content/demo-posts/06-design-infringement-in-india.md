Designs protect how things look. Not how they work, not what they are called, not the creative idea behind them. Only the visual features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament, or composition of lines or colours, applied to an article by an industrial process, which appeal to and are judged solely by the eye. Everything about the registered design system follows from that narrow definition, and most of the confusion in this area comes from forgetting it.

## What a registration gives

Registration gives the proprietor, for a limited term, the exclusive right to apply the design to the article in the class for which it is registered. Unlike copyright, the right does not depend on proof of copying. Unlike a trade mark, it does not depend on reputation or use. A defendant who independently arrives at the same appearance still infringes; a proprietor who has never sold a single unit still has a right to enforce.

The counterpart is that the right is only as good as the registration. A design that was not new or original when filed, or that had been published before, is liable to be cancelled, and cancellation is a routine defence to an infringement claim. The proprietor's case therefore has two fronts from the outset: that the registration is valid, and that the defendant has taken it.

## Novelty and originality

To be registrable a design must be new or original, not previously published in India or elsewhere, and not significantly distinguishable from a design already known. Novelty is judged against the prior art of published designs and articles; originality speaks to the designer's own contribution rather than to invention.

The difficulty in practice is one of degree. Almost every design draws on what came before. A chair is a chair. The question is whether the combination of features, taken as a whole, would strike the eye as new, or whether the differences are mere trade variants, the sort of minor changes that any maker in the trade might make without any design effort. Courts have consistently refused to treat trivial variations as new designs, and a proprietor whose registration rests on such variations should expect it to be attacked.

## What is excluded

The definition excludes several things that clients often expect it to cover:

- **Mode or principle of construction.** How the article is put together is not a design.
- **Purely functional features.** A feature dictated solely by the function the article performs is not protected, though a feature that is functional and *also* has eye-appeal may be.
- **Trade marks and property marks.** A design cannot be used as a back door to register what is really a brand.
- **Artistic works** protected as such by copyright.

The last exclusion is where design law and copyright law meet, and where the overlap must be handled carefully.

## The eye of the customer

Infringement, which the statute calls piracy, is committed by applying the registered design, or a fraudulent or obvious imitation of it, to an article in the registered class without licence. The comparison is visual. The court looks at the registered design and at the defendant's article and asks whether the latter is the same or an obvious imitation.

Through whose eyes? The question is more contested than it looks. The traditional answer is the eye of the customer, the person who buys articles of this kind, who is taken to notice features that would strike such a purchaser and to overlook those that would not. Some decisions have preferred a more instructed eye, familiar with the prior art, on the footing that in a crowded field small differences matter more. In practice the two converge. Where the prior art is dense, the registration's novelty lies in its details, and only the details can be infringed. Where the design is a real departure, a defendant who takes the departure will not be saved by trivial changes.

> The comparison is between the registered design and the defendant's article, not between the two parties' products. A proprietor who has drifted from their own registration may find that the defendant's article is closer to the market product than to the right.

## The overlap with copyright

A design drawing is an artistic work. The article made from it may be a registrable design. The two regimes cannot both be exploited at full strength, and Indian law resolves the tension in two ways.

First, an artistic work that is registered as a design loses copyright protection as an artistic work. Second, and this is the trap, an artistic work that is *capable* of registration as a design but has not been registered loses copyright once the article has been reproduced more than a modest number of times (fifty, under the present statute) by an industrial process. A manufacturer who relies on copyright in the drawings to stop a copier of a mass-produced product will usually find that the copyright has gone, and that the design, having never been registered, gives nothing either.

The practical rule for anyone commercialising a product's appearance is to register the design before launch. The practical rule for anyone defending a claim is to ask, before anything else, which regime the claimant is actually in.

## Passing off

A registered design cannot be a trade mark, but the appearance of a product can acquire a reputation, and the common law protects reputation through passing off. So a claimant whose design registration is weak or expired may still restrain a copier whose product is likely to be taken for theirs, provided they can prove goodwill in the shape or get-up, which is harder than proving goodwill in a name.

Whether design infringement and passing off can be combined in a single suit has been debated, on the reasoning that the two rights rest on different foundations. The weight of recent authority permits the composite suit, but the causes of action remain distinct, and the evidence required for each is different. A well-drafted plaint keeps them apart.[^1]
