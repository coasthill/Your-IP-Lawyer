The action for passing off is older than the registration system it now lives beside. It is, at heart, a tort: nobody may represent their goods or services as those of another. Registration is irrelevant to it. What matters is *reputation* — and what a member of the public is likely to believe when confronted with the defendant's mark in the market rather than in a courtroom.

## The classical trinity

Courts in India have consistently framed passing off around three elements, often called the classical trinity:

1. **Goodwill or reputation** attached to the claimant's goods or services in the minds of the purchasing public, associated with the identifying get-up (a name, a mark, a trade dress).
2. **Misrepresentation** by the defendant to the public, whether or not intentional, that is likely to lead the public to believe that the defendant's goods or services are those of the claimant.
3. **Damage**, or the likelihood of damage, to the claimant's goodwill by reason of the misrepresentation.

Each element carries evidentiary weight. Goodwill is proved with sales, advertising, market presence and, where available, consumer perception. Misrepresentation is inferred from similarity, trade channels and the class of customer. Damage is often presumed once the first two are established, but a claimant who cannot articulate *how* they are hurt gives the court an easy exit.

## Similarity is judged by the wrong person, deliberately

The most common mistake in a passing off argument is to compare the two marks side by side and list their differences. That is precisely what the law says not to do.

The test is that of the *unwary purchaser of average intelligence and imperfect recollection*. The customer does not carry the claimant's mark into the shop; they carry a memory of it. The question is whether the defendant's mark is likely to trigger that memory and cause confusion, not whether an expert with both products on the table could tell them apart.

Several consequences follow:

- **Overall impression** governs. Marks are compared as wholes. Dissecting a mark into elements and comparing the elements is a recognised error.
- **Phonetic similarity** can be enough on its own, particularly for goods that are ordered by name, such as medicines.
- **Idea of the mark** matters. Two marks that convey the same idea through different words or images can be deceptively similar.
- **Added matter** rarely cures deception. A defendant who adopts the claimant's essential feature and adds a house name may make matters worse, not better, by suggesting a connection.

## Where the argument usually goes wrong

In practice, passing off claims tend to fail for one of three reasons.

**First**, the claimant proves *use* but not *goodwill*. Filing invoices is not the same as showing that the public associates the mark with a single source. This is especially acute for descriptive or laudatory words, where the claimant must show that the word has acquired a secondary meaning.

**Second**, the claimant treats registration as if it settled the matter. In a composite suit for infringement and passing off, the two causes of action run on different rails. A registered proprietor may still lose a passing off claim if the defendant's goodwill predates their own in a particular territory.

**Third**, the claimant argues the differences. Counsel who spend their time explaining why the marks are distinguishable have usually already conceded that the similarities need explaining.

## A note on interim relief

Because passing off protects reputation, and reputation erodes quickly, most of the action happens at the interim stage. The claimant must show a prima facie case, that the balance of convenience favours restraint, and that damages would not be an adequate remedy. Delay is fatal more often than parties expect: a claimant who has watched the defendant trade for years will struggle to explain why the harm is suddenly irreparable.

## The point of the exercise

Similarity is not a property of two marks; it is a property of the relationship between two marks and a market. The same pair of names may be deceptively similar in one trade and comfortably distinct in another. The law asks the question through the eyes of the person most likely to be deceived, and the advocate who forgets that will find themselves defending the wrong case.[^1]
