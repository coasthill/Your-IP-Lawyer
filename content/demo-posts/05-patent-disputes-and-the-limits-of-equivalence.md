A patent is not a monopoly over an idea, a product or a field. It is a monopoly over what the claims say. Everything else in the document, the description, the drawings, the examples, exists to teach the invention and to help a reader understand the claims. The claims themselves are the fence. A defendant who stays outside the fence does not infringe, however close they come; a defendant who steps inside does, however clever the disguise.

That sounds simple. The difficulty is that fences are drawn in words, words have edges, and inventors rarely anticipate every way in which a competitor might approach the boundary.

## Reading the claim

Infringement analysis begins with construction. The court reads the claim as a person skilled in the art would have read it at the relevant date, using the specification as context, and settles what each term means. This is a question for the court, not a question of what the patentee now wishes the claim to mean, and it happens before any comparison with the defendant's product.

Construction is *purposive* rather than purely literal. The court asks what the patentee, addressing skilled readers, would have been understood to intend by the language used. That is not a licence to rewrite the claim, but a recognition that technical language is read by technical people. A claim to a "resilient member" is not confined to the exact material shown in the drawings if the skilled reader would understand the term more generally; nor is it expanded to cover rigid components merely because the invention would work with them.

## Literal infringement

Once construed, the claim is broken into its elements, the integers, and the defendant's product or process is examined for each. If every integer is present, there is literal infringement. If any integer is missing, there is no literal infringement, and the analysis moves to the harder question.

This is the *all-elements* principle, and it is worth stating plainly because it is so often skipped in argument. It is not enough that the defendant's product does the same job, achieves the same result or was plainly inspired by the patent. It must have each of the features the patentee chose to claim.

## Beyond the literal: pith and marrow, and equivalents

No system of patent law confines infringement to the literal. A defendant who substitutes a trivially different component for one integer, a screw for a rivet or an interchangeable solvent for the one named, would otherwise escape every claim, and patents would be drafted to absurd lengths trying to anticipate every substitution.

Two doctrines address this, and they come from different traditions.

The older English approach asks whether the defendant has taken the *pith and marrow* of the invention: its essential features, as distinct from inessential variants. An integer that is essential must be present in some form; an inessential integer may be varied or omitted without escaping infringement. The doctrine turns, therefore, on the prior question of which integers are essential, and that question is answered by reading the claim purposively.

The American doctrine of *equivalents* asks, for each integer not literally present, whether the defendant's substitute performs substantially the same function, in substantially the same way, to achieve substantially the same result. If so, the substitute is equivalent and the integer is treated as present.

Indian courts have drawn on both vocabularies. The pith and marrow language has a longer history in our jurisprudence; the language of equivalents appears increasingly in recent decisions. Whether the two approaches produce different results in practice, and which the courts will ultimately settle on, remains contestable. What can be said with some confidence is that the enquiry is always element by element, and that courts have been unreceptive to the argument that a defendant infringes because their product is, in some overall sense, "like" the invention.

> Equivalence is a doctrine about substitutes for particular features. It is not a doctrine about resemblance.

## The limits

Equivalence has boundaries, and the defendant's case usually lives at one of them.

- **The prior art.** A claim cannot be stretched by equivalence to cover what was already known. If the defendant's product is simply the prior art, it cannot infringe a valid claim; and if the claim, read to cover it, would also cover the prior art, the claim is invalid.
- **Deliberate limitation.** Where the patentee chose narrow language when broader language was available, the court is slow to restore by equivalence what the patentee gave up by drafting.
- **The file wrapper.** Statements made to the Patent Office to secure grant, whether narrowing amendments or arguments distinguishing prior art, are part of the public record. A patentee who told the examiner that the claim did *not* cover a particular variant will find it difficult to argue in court that it does. India has no codified doctrine of prosecution history estoppel, and how much weight the file should carry is a matter of ongoing debate. But the prudent assumption is that the file will be read, and that inconsistency will be noticed.
- **Essential integers.** An integer that the skilled reader would regard as essential to the invention cannot be omitted on the footing that the defendant achieves the result some other way. That is not equivalence; that is a different invention.

## The two-front war

A defendant sued for patent infringement almost never fights on infringement alone. The usual answer is twofold: the product does not fall within the claims properly construed; and, if it does, the claims are invalid for want of novelty or inventive step, for insufficiency, or because they claim excluded subject-matter. The two arguments pull in opposite directions. A broad construction helps the infringement case and hurts validity, and the patentee has to hold a single construction that survives both.

This is the central tension of patent litigation. The claim must be read wide enough to catch the defendant and narrow enough to avoid the prior art. Equivalence widens the reach; it also widens the exposure. Advocates who reach for equivalence too early sometimes discover that they have argued their client's patent out of validity.[^1]
